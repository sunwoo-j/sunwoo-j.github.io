## What is Jakob’s Law?

Usability specialist [Jakob Nielsen](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/end-of-web-design/) described Jakob’s Law in 2000. Applied more broadly to interfaces, its idea can be paraphrased as follows:

> People spend much of their time using other products, so they expect a new product to behave in familiar ways.

Users do not want to learn every interface from scratch. They bring expectations about structure, layout, and interaction from services they already use.

## Why do familiar patterns help?

Understanding a new structure requires interpretation and adds cognitive effort. A familiar structure can reduce that effort. Examples include:

- Selecting the logo to return home
- Looking for primary navigation in a bottom tab bar
- Pressing Escape to open a pause menu
- Using WASD to move a game character

People who have used those environments can anticipate these behaviors from experience. Familiarity differs across devices, cultures, and levels of experience, so the audience still matters. Familiar patterns are a way to reduce unnecessary learning effort.

## Innovation and learning effort

What if we want an interface to feel new?

A completely new UI can be original, but it can also ask users to learn more. The useful question is:

> Does this change offer enough value to justify the learning effort?

Breaking a familiar pattern simply to look different can come at a usability cost.

## Implications for interface design

Jakob’s Law does not mean every design must be conservative. A useful approach is to keep the basic structure familiar and introduce changes where they serve a purpose.

A change inside a familiar framework can be easier to learn than replacing the framework itself.

## A short design checklist

- Is this a pattern users encounter in other products?
- Can the intended audience understand it without additional explanation?
- Does the new behavior solve a real problem?
- Was it changed only to make it look different?

## Closing thoughts

People may be less opposed to new things than to unnecessary learning. Familiarity can help reduce that burden.

Jakob’s Law asks not only whether to make something new, but how much of it needs to be new.
